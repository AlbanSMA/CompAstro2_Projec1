\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Two galaxies passing by each other is an incredibly complex event. The gravitational pull of one galaxy on the other, and the pull of the components of the galaxies between themselves, create complex structures such as tidal streams and can change the path and velocity of both galaxies. Due to the length of the event, they are also impossible to observe in their entirety. Modelling them is thus the best way to understand the circumstances and evolution of these events. Complex simulations even allow for the rewinding of one particular event to understand what led to a known object. The size and complexity of the event also prevent analytical solutions which would explain the evolution of the objects accurately from working. However, it is possible to create simple models which can run on any machine and are sufficiently accurate for most needs. They can be modelled with algorithms involving only the gravitational pull from collisionless particle interactions. While these models do not reproduce small scale or particular events, they show the broader evolution of galaxies after a flyby depending on the characteristics of the galaxies involved. The model for galaxy interaction presented here is such a model.


On the other hand, because it happens on a much smaller scale, the formation of a single star necessitates a slightly more complex modelling. This is necessary even for basic models which show only the broader elements of star formation. Stars form when some regions in molecular clouds become dense enough and collapse on themselves. If only the gravitational pull is taken into account, the cloud will collapse into denser objects then stars are. With both gravity and a pressure gradient, the particles move inward due to gravity, until the pressure gradient pushes back. This expansion and collapse continues indefinitely without a third force to dissipate the excess energy. Thus, to accurately model a star forming, viscosity must also be taken into account, as a function of velocity. The particles collapse inward and rebound in turn, slowly dissipating energy until they settle in hydrostatic equilibrium, at the points where gravity and pressure balance.


This project is composed of two parts. In the first part, we will implement a numerical model of a moving galaxy flying by a second stationary galaxy, testing it with different size of galaxies and different offsets. In the second part, we will model the formation of a star. Both models use N-body simulations, with different numbers of particles for each models, but the same amount within one model for each tests. 

\section{Implementation}\label{sec:imp}

Both models were based on the same structure. A main file called forward multiple functions to initiate, evolve and plot the galaxies and stars.

The first set of functions placed N particles in a circle around a centre of mass (x, y) with a normal distribution for the radius, and a uniform distribution for the angle. It calculated an initial velocity in the x and y directions using 0.4 of a Keplerian velocity for the galaxies, which is dependent on the radius from the centre of mass. This was taken as zero for the stars. Then the acceleration of each particle was calculated from this initial velocity and the position of all the other particles in the simulation. A second set of functions updated the positions, velocity and acceleration of the particles after a time step dt using a leapfrog algorithm. It first calculated an updated velocity after half a step using the previous acceleration, then calculated an updated position using this new velocity, before getting the updated acceleration and taking the second half-step in velocity using this new acceleration. This is the leap-frog algorithm:
\begin{verbatim}
    new_v = v + dt/2 * acc
    new_p = p + dt * new_v
    new_acc = getAcceleration(*args)
    new_new_v = new_v + dt/2 * new_acc
\end{verbatim}
Where getAcceleration() calculates the acceleration of the particles using the different equations discussed below. A last set of functions created and updated the plots, making a series of image every few time steps.

\subsection{Equation of motion}
The equation of motion was the most complex part of this model, as it had to be calculated individually for each particles. It was present in different ways in the two models, and this was the largest difference between them. As mentioned in the \nameref{sec:intro}, one of the model only included gravitational effects, while the other included both gravitational effects, a pressure gradient, and viscosity. The gravitational effects on one particle were a weighted sum of the pull of the other particles as a function of their mass and distance to the particle of interest:
$$\vec{F_j} = m_j G \sum_{i \neq j} \frac{m_i}{r^3_{ji}} \vec{r_{ij}} = m_j \vec{a_j} \Leftrightarrow \vec{a_j} = G \sum_{i \neq j} \frac{m_i}{r^3_{ji}}\vec{r_{ij}}.$$

The pressure gradient was slightly more complex, as it needed to be calculated as a function of the average density around the particle of interest instead of its interaction with the other individual particles. Each particle in the model is a point particle which represents a spread of particles with a maximum density in the centre of the spread. So we take the density at each points in space as a sum of the densities from each modelled particles. These densities vary with distance to the modelled particle and go down to zero beyond a certain point. Thus, the density at each point depends on the distance from that point to every particle in the model. At a location r, it is:

$$\rho(\vec{r}) = \sum_i m_i W_i(|\vec{r} - \vec{r_i}|, h).$$

Where $W_i$ is the smoothing kernel which defines the spread, and is:

\begin{equation}
  W_i(q,h) = C \begin{cases}
    (2-q)^3 - 4(1-q^3)&\text{if $1 > q\geq0$,}\\
    (2-q)^3 & \text{if $2>q\geq1$,}\\
    0 & \text{if $q\geq 2$.}
  \end{cases}
\end{equation}

$C=\frac{5}{14\pi h^2}$, $q_i=\frac{|\vec{r} - \vec{r_i}|}{h}$ and h are constants. We chose h to be $0.02\cdot R_\odot$ for most simulations, this defines the size of the density spread. From it, and assuming an isothermal equation of state, it is possible to obtain the pressure : $P=\kappa\rho^2$, and from that the pressure gradient:

$$\vec{\nabla} P(\vec{r_j}) = 2\kappa\sum_im_i\vec{\nabla}_jW(|\vec{r_j}-\vec{r_i}|,h)$$

Finally, the impact of viscosity on each particle is simply a constant $\nu$ multiplied by the velocity of the particle at time t. The exact values used in this report for the different models can be found in \nameref{sec:AppA} and \nameref{sec:AppB}.

\subsection{Acceleration of the code}
As an N-body simulation is extremely taxing for the machine running it, multiple methods were used to accelerate the process, and make it less taxing. There were two main possible paths. The first one was to use the Numba package and compile the Python code into a language which the machine has less difficulty running. It also included the parallelisation of some parts of the code. In particular, \textit{for loops} are extremely slow in Python. Running multiple of the loops parallelly accelerated the code significantly. The second method was to entirely erase the need for \textit{for loops} by using NumPy array maths. 

The code which removes the need for \textit{for loops} entirely using the array maths is reproduced here:
\begin{verbatim}
  p_sub_0 = np.repeat(p[0,:], len(p[0,:]), axis=0).reshape(len(p[0,:]), 
                            len(p[0,:]))
  p_sub_1 = np.repeat(p[1,:], len(p[1,:]), axis=0).reshape(len(p[1,:]), 
                            len(p[1,:]))
  r_ji_x = p[0,:].transpose()-p_sub_0
  r_ji_y = p[1,:].transpose()-p_sub_1

  #Clip anything too close to 0
  r_ji = np.clip(np.sqrt((r_ji_x)**2 + (r_ji_y)**2), 0.1*Rg, None)

  #Get the acceleration
  acc[0,:] = - G * sum((mp[:] / (r_ji)**3)*r_ji_x)
  acc[1,:] = - G * sum((mp[:] / (r_ji)**3)*r_ji_y)
\end{verbatim}

It creates a larger matrix of size \emph{number of particle} $\times$ \emph{number of particles}, such that when calculating the resulting radius, it should produce this sequence of arrays:
$$r_{ji,x} = \begin{bmatrix}
  p_{x1}\\
  p_{x2}\\
  p_{x3}\\
\end{bmatrix} - \begin{bmatrix}
  p_{x1} & p_{x2} & p_{x3}\\
  p_{x1} & p_{x2} & p_{x3}\\
  p_{x1} & p_{x2} & p_{x3}\\
\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}
  p_{x1} - p_{x1} & p_{x1} - p_{x2} & p_{x1} - p_{x3}\\
  p_{x2} - p_{x1} & p_{x2} - p_{x2} & p_{x2} - p_{x3}\\
  p_{x3} - p_{x1} & p_{x3} - p_{x2} & p_{x3} - p_{x3}\\
\end{bmatrix}$$
Then:
$$r_{ji} = \sqrt{\begin{bmatrix}
  p_{x1} - p_{x1} & p_{x1} - p_{x2} & p_{x1} - p_{x3}\\
  p_{x2} - p_{x1} & p_{x2} - p_{x2} & p_{x2} - p_{x3}\\
  p_{x3} - p_{x1} & p_{x3} - p_{x2} & p_{x3} - p_{x3}\\
\end{bmatrix}^2 + \begin{bmatrix}
  p_{y1} - p_{y1} & p_{y1} - p_{y2} & p_{y1} - p_{y3}\\
  p_{y2} - p_{y1} & p_{y2} - p_{y2} & p_{y2} - p_{y3}\\
  p_{y3} - p_{y1} & p_{y3} - p_{y2} & p_{y3} - p_{y3}\\
\end{bmatrix}^2}$$

Which produces an n by n matrix where each row is the radius between a particle and all the others. Finally, the calculation of the acceleration sums over the entire row, summing over the contribution of each particle i on the acceleration of particle j.\newline

The test of the comparative speeds of the three methods was made using the galaxy model, and a smaller simulation, with only 500 particles in one galaxy, and 250 in the other. The three methods being: with no acceleration, using Numba, and using array maths. The fastest was the Numba method, at 529.2 seconds, followed by the array method at 1309 seconds, more than double the time. As expected, the method with no acceleration was last, taking nearly 1500 seconds. So, while compiling the code in Numba and running multiple loops in parallel is the best option, reducing the number of \textit{for loops} did significantly improve the running of the simulation. The large matrix it has to build and the plotting end up being the longer operation instead of the loops, and they take less time.

There are probably ways in which the no loops methods could be improved but it does work as a proof of concept. These methods were only implemented in the galaxy interaction model but not in the star formation model. The Numba method did not work in the second because functions were called within the loops. NumPy arrays were used as much as possible, but because the code was more complex and for readability, the \textit{for loops} could not be entirely removed. Arrays were nonetheless used as much as possible without creating the large matrices mentioned above.

\subsection{Galaxy Merger}

Using the galaxy merger model, four different simulations were run. The parameters used for all of them can be found in \nameref{sec:AppA}. The first one had a Milky Way size galaxy ($1.54\cdot10^{12}M_\odot$) interacting with an extremely low mass galaxy, of only $10^5$ times the mass of the Sun. The second was interacting with a larger galaxy, although still low mass, of about $10^{10}$ the mass of the Sun. The third also included a change in mass, with two galaxies of about equal mass interacting. The last was slightly different in that it included the Milky Way size galaxy and the $10^{10}M_\odot$ ones, but with a head-on collision instead of the offset fly-by which the others modelled.\newline

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:dis}

As mentioned in the \nameref{sec:intro}, numerical simulations are necessary to understand phenomena which can not be observed in their entirety and are too complex for analytical solutions. Although quite basic, the simulations in this report already represent the phenomena they model quite well. 

Concerning their accuracy, the results within the galaxy merger simulation depend heavily on the initial radial velocity of the particles. Giving them too much velocity at first will make them fly away instead of rotating around the centre of mass of the galaxy. This means that the galaxies will disperse before they interact, or that the interaction can be impacted by this too high initial velocity. It is also an extremely simplified model, which only include the gravitational interaction of heavy collisionless particles. More complex models show that some collision are necessary to describe accurately the evolution of galaxies. Furthermore, due to the limit imposed by the machine it is run on, the simulations have to be low resolution. Each increase in the number of particles proportionally increases the time it takes the simulations to run, and the power it takes to run them. Most portable computers are uncapable of running more complex models. Using Numba or reducing and removing for loops and plotting routines do improve this point, but neither can improve it indefinitely. 


The stellar model is slightly more complex, and due to the smaller size of the objects being modelled, it can be simulated in higher resolution by using the same number of particles as within the galaxy simulations. They took longer than the galaxy simulations because it was only accelerated through some array maths instead of Numba. It is not possible to estimate the exact time it took for the simulations to run because some of them were paused, or not stopped in time, but the longest one took 7h with a pause, and the average was closer to 2h. They also used more particles for most of the simulations. The model also makes some assumptions which could impact the results. In particular, it does not take into account magnetic fields, which are known to be very strong in star formation discs and young stars. It also assumes an isothermal model, which does not take changes in temperature into account. Temperature is important in star formation, as it is one of the variables in the calculation of Jeans' mass. This mass determines whether a molecular cloud can collapse into a star or not. Because of these two factors in particular, this model could represent the formation of a planet, or of any other object which collapse from a cloud of particle into a sphere just as well as a star. The results also depend on the initial position of the particles, as if the standard deviation of the normal distribution which defines their distance to the centre is too small, the initial pressure gradient is too large, and they are ejected. The results also depend on the smoothing factor $h$, which changes both the radius and the distance from each others at which the particles settle. It might be possible to model a binary star from this model, by using the same basis as for the simulation with two centre of masses and placing them further away form each others and with an initial velocity.


Finally, both models are made in closed box simulations, where only the initial particles and objects interact. This is not an entirely accurate representation, especially for the stellar formation model. The galaxies in the galaxy merger model are large enough and far enough away from other heavy objects that they should not be overly impacted. However, stars form in groups with other stars, and are surrounded by many other elements. In these closed box model, all the particles end up in the star after a certain time, instead of some of them escaping or being pulled away.


In conclusion, both these simulations are accurate models of the astrophysical phenomena they aim to represent. However, multiple effects which have been shown to impact them were not included in the simulations. Thus, while they can be used to understand the movements of large structures, the minute details can not be understood with their help.\newline
